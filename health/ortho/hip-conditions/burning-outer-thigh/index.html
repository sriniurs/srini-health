MERALGIA PARESTHETICA

Why does the outer part of my thigh burn and feel numb?

Why a pinched skin nerve causes burning on the outer thigh, why tight waistbands and weight matter, and how it settles.

OUTER THIGHTIGHT WAISTBANDSUSUALLY SETTLES

About 5 minutes to read · Patient education

01

A burning patch on the thigh

Girija is an invented example. She is 48, a school cook in Davangere with type 2 diabetes. She has gained weight over the past two years. For three months the outer front of her right thigh has burned and tingled, and a palm-sized patch of skin feels numb, as if it belongs to someone else. Her sari petticoat feels tight at the waist, and the burning is worse when she stands for long periods at the stove. Walking is fine, and her hip moves normally.

The short answer: this is meralgia paresthetica, a pinched skin nerve at the front of the hip. It causes burning, tingling and numbness on the outer thigh, but no weakness. Tight waistbands, weight gain, pregnancy and diabetes are common causes. It usually settles with looser clothing and weight loss, and rarely needs injections or surgery. [2][3]

02

What's happening in my thigh?

The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve carries feeling from the skin of the outer thigh. It passes from the abdomen into the thigh just inside the bony point at the front of the pelvis, under a tight band in the groin. Pressure at this point squeezes the nerve. [2]

Because this nerve only carries feeling, it causes burning, tingling and numbness, but never weakness. The hip joint itself is normal.

03

Why might it happen to me?

Anything that presses on the nerve at the front of the hip can cause it: tight belts, tight trousers, tight waistbands, weight gain, pregnancy, fluid in the abdomen and heavy belts. Diabetes makes nerves more vulnerable. [2][3]

In India, a tightly tied petticoat or salwar cord, worn at the same spot every day, can press on the waist and front of the hip. Indian doctors have described skin damage from tight drawstrings, and the same pressure can affect nearby nerves. [1]

04

What will the clinician check?

Your doctor will map the numb area, check for weakness and reflexes in the leg, examine your back and hip, and press near the front of the pelvis to see if it reproduces the tingling. Normal strength and reflexes point to meralgia rather than a disc or nerve root problem.

Tests aren't usually needed. Blood sugar and thyroid tests make sense if not recently done. If there's any doubt, nerve tests or scans can rule out other causes, such as a disc problem or something pressing in the pelvis.

THE DEEP DIVEWhat helps, and what to skip
05

What can I do?

Loosen up. Wear the petticoat or salwar cord looser and tie it at a slightly different place, avoid tight belts and low tight trousers, and choose elastic or wider waistbands. [1][2]

Lose weight if needed. Even modest weight loss reduces pressure on the nerve. [3]

Control sugar. Good diabetes control helps nerves recover.

In a series of 12 people with meralgia from tight trousers, all were treated successfully without surgery by avoiding tight trousers, a local steroid injection when needed, and weight loss. [3]

06

Do medicines or injections help?

Most people don't need medicines. If burning is troublesome, nerve-calming medicines can help. A local anaesthetic and steroid injection near the nerve at the front of the pelvis can relieve symptoms and confirm the diagnosis. [3]

Surgery to free or cut the nerve is used rarely, only for severe symptoms that don't respond. [2]

07

Will it come back?

It can return if the pressure returns, for example with weight gain, pregnancy or going back to tight waistbands. Keeping waistbands loose and weight steady helps prevent it.

08

When should I get help sooner?

Call 112 (or 108 for an ambulance), or go to an emergency department, if:

  • new weakness of the leg, numbness in the groin or around the back passage, or loss of bladder or bowel control

These suggest serious nerve compression in the spine, not meralgia.

See a doctor soon if numbness spreads below the knee, if the leg feels weak, or if you have back pain shooting down the leg.

Routine review makes sense if outer thigh burning persists after a few weeks of looser clothing.

Girija loosens her petticoat and ties it a little higher, starts walking in the evenings and improves her sugar control. Over four months the burning goes, leaving a small numb patch that no longer bothers her.

Burning and numbness on the outer thigh with no weakness is usually meralgia paresthetica, a pinched skin nerve. Loosen tight waistbands and cords, lose weight if needed and control sugar. Most cases settle without injections or surgery. [1][2][3]
